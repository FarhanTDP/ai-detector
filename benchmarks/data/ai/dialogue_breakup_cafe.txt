"You're late," Nina said, not looking up from her coffee.

"Eleven minutes." Tom slid into the chair across from her. "The bridge was up. Some guy in a sailboat who clearly had nowhere to be."

"You always have a reason."

"I always have a true reason. There's a difference."

She finally looked at him. He'd gotten a haircut, a bad one, slightly uneven above the left ear. A month ago she would have told him. A month ago she would have fixed it herself with the kitchen scissors.

"I ordered you a flat white," she said. "It's probably cold."

"It's fine." He took a sip and made a face. "It's not fine. It's terrible. Thank you, though."

They sat for a moment. At the next table, a toddler was methodically dropping pieces of a muffin onto the floor while his father scrolled through his phone.

"So," Tom said. "The apartment."

"I'll keep it through the end of the lease. You can take the couch. You paid for most of it anyway."

"I don't want the couch."

"Tom."

"I'm serious. Where would I put it? I'm sleeping in my brother's office on a futon that smells like printer ink." He turned the cup in a slow circle on its saucer. "Keep the couch. Keep the weird lamp. I just want the record player and the cast iron pan."

"The pan was my grandmother's."

"The other pan."

"Oh." She almost smiled. "The one you ruined."

"Seasoned. The one I seasoned."

The toddler ran out of muffin and began to cry. His father picked him up without looking away from the screen, and the crying stopped.

"I keep thinking I'll be angrier," Nina said quietly. "I had a whole speech. I practiced it in the car."

"Do you want to give it? I can sit here and take it. I've got at least until the meter runs out."

"No. It was mostly about the dishes, in the end. Isn't that stupid?"

"It's not stupid."

"Four years, and my big closing argument is that you never rinsed a bowl."

Tom looked down at his terrible coffee. "I'd rinse it now," he said. "For whatever that's worth."

"I know," she said. "That's the worst part."
