My grandmother never measured anything. This drove my mother crazy, and it's the reason I still can't make her bread.

She kept the flour in a blue tin with a dent in the lid, and she'd scoop it out with a chipped teacup that had lost its handle sometime before I was born. "About this much," she would say, tipping a heap onto the wooden board. Then she'd look at it, frown, and add a little more. Salt was a pinch between three fingers. Water was "until it feels right." When I asked how I was supposed to know when it felt right, she looked at me as though I'd asked how to know when I was hungry.

I spent most of the summer I was nine in that kitchen. My parents were sorting out the end of their marriage, though nobody had told me that yet, and I'd been sent to stay with her "for the fresh air." There wasn't much fresh air. The kitchen was small and hot and smelled like yeast and the gas stove, and the window over the sink looked out on a brick wall and a single stubborn fig tree.

She didn't talk much while she worked, and she never asked me how I was feeling, for which I was grateful. Instead she gave me jobs. I greased the pans. I stood on a stool and watched the dough rise under a striped towel, which is not really a job, but she treated it like one. "Tell me when it's doubled," she'd say, and I would stare at it with enormous seriousness for an hour.

I've tried to write the recipe down a dozen times since she died. I've bought a kitchen scale. I've watched videos. The bread I make is fine. People eat it and say nice things. But the crust is wrong, and the inside is too even, and it doesn't taste like that summer.

A few years ago my mother found the teacup in a box and mailed it to me, wrapped in a dish towel. It sits on a shelf above my stove now. I've never used it. I think I'm afraid I'll scoop the flour, add a little more, and find out that the cup was never the secret.
