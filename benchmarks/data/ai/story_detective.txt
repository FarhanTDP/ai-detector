Detective Marcus Hale had seen plenty of strange cases in his twenty years on the force, but none quite like the disappearance of the Whitmore diamond.

The gem had been locked inside a glass case in the center of the Harrington Museum's main gallery. The room had no windows, only one door, and three security cameras that recorded every inch of the space. Yet when the curator arrived on Monday morning, the case was intact, the alarm had never sounded, and the diamond was simply gone.

Marcus walked slowly around the display, his footsteps echoing in the silent hall. The curator, a nervous man named Theodore Price, hovered at his side, wringing his hands.

"I don't understand it, Detective," Price said. "Nobody could have gotten in. The footage shows nothing. It's as if the diamond vanished into thin air."

Marcus crouched and examined the base of the case. The glass was spotless, almost too spotless. He ran a gloved finger along the edge and frowned. There was a faint residue, slightly sticky to the touch.

"When was this case last cleaned?" he asked.

Price hesitated. "Friday evening, I believe. Our cleaning staff comes in after closing."

"And who has access to the security footage?"

"Only myself and the head of security, Ms. Delgado."

Marcus nodded thoughtfully. He asked to see the recordings from Friday night. As the footage played, he watched the cleaning crew move through the gallery, polishing floors and wiping down surfaces. Everything appeared normal. Then, at 11:47 p.m., the image flickered for less than a second.

"Did you see that?" Marcus asked.

Price leaned closer to the monitor. "See what?"

Marcus rewound the footage and played it again, frame by frame. For a single moment, the timestamp jumped from 11:47:12 to 11:47:58. Forty-six seconds were missing.

"Someone edited this recording," Marcus said quietly. "And whoever did it knew exactly what they were doing."

The curator's face went pale. "But only two people have access to the system."

Marcus stood and turned toward him, his expression calm but unwavering. "That's right, Mr. Price. And one of them has been sweating since the moment I walked in."

For a long moment, the curator said nothing. Then his shoulders sagged, and he let out a shaky breath. "I needed the money," he whispered. "The museum was going to let me go. I thought no one would ever know."

Marcus pulled out his notebook and began to write. In his experience, the most complicated mysteries often had the simplest explanations. People rarely vanished into thin air, and neither did diamonds. They simply ended up in the hands of someone who believed they would never be caught.

As the officers led Price away, Marcus took one last look at the empty glass case. Somewhere out there, the Whitmore diamond was waiting to be found, and he intended to find it.
