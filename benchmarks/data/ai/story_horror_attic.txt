The scratching started on the third night after we moved into the house on Alder Street.

At first, I told myself it was the wind. The house was old, built sometime in the 1890s, and every board seemed to groan and settle as the temperature dropped. But wind does not have rhythm. And the sound coming from the attic above my bedroom had a rhythm, slow and deliberate, like fingernails dragging across wood. Three long scrapes. A pause. Then three more.

My husband, Daniel, insisted it was mice. "Old houses have critters," he said, rolling over and pulling the blanket over his head. "We'll call an exterminator in the morning."

The exterminator came on Thursday. He spent nearly an hour in the attic and came down shaking his head. No droppings, no nests, no chewed insulation. "Whatever you're hearing, ma'am," he said, wiping the dust from his hands, "it isn't rodents."

That night, the scratching was louder.

I lay awake, staring at the ceiling, counting the scrapes. Three. Pause. Three. Pause. Around two in the morning, the pattern changed. The scratching moved, slowly, from the center of the ceiling toward the corner of the room, directly above the closet. Then it stopped entirely.

The silence was worse.

I do not know what possessed me to get up. Maybe I simply needed to know. I took the flashlight from the nightstand, careful not to wake Daniel, and pulled down the narrow folding ladder in the hallway. The air in the attic was cold, far colder than it should have been, and it smelled faintly of damp earth and something sweeter, like flowers left too long in a vase.

My flashlight beam swept across boxes left by the previous owners, an old rocking horse, a dressmaker's mannequin with no head. Then the light landed on the wooden beam above the closet. Carved deep into the wood, in clumsy, uneven letters, were three words.

LET ME OUT.

I stumbled backward, my heart hammering against my ribs. And that was when I noticed the small wooden door set low into the attic wall, a door that had not been there when the exterminator searched. A door secured with an old iron latch.

From the other side, very softly, came three slow scratches.

Then a voice, small and tired, like a child who had been waiting for a very long time.

"Please," it whispered. "You're the first one who listened."
