The rain started the afternoon my father forgot my name.

It was not dramatic. There was no shattered glass, no doctor delivering news in a hushed voice. We were sitting at the kitchen table, the same scarred oak table where he had taught me long division and later, much later, how to fill out a tax return. He was holding a cup of tea that had long since gone cold, and he looked up at me with a polite, slightly puzzled smile, the kind you give a stranger who has sat down beside you on a train.

"I'm sorry," he said. "Have we met?"

Outside, the first drops began to tap against the window. I remember thinking how ordinary the sound was, how indifferent. The world, it seemed, had not been informed that something enormous had just happened in this small yellow kitchen.

I told him my name. He repeated it carefully, as though tasting a word in a foreign language, and nodded. Then he went back to his tea.

In the weeks that followed, I learned that memory does not disappear all at once. It erodes, like a shoreline, a little more with each tide. Some days he knew me. Some days he believed I was his sister, who had died in 1987. Once, memorably, he mistook me for the man who came to read the gas meter and spent twenty minutes explaining that the pipes in the basement had always been temperamental.

I stopped correcting him after a while. It seemed cruel to keep insisting on a reality that only made him anxious. If he wanted me to be his sister, I would be his sister. If he wanted me to be the gas man, I would nod and pretend to write something down on an imaginary clipboard.

What surprised me most was not the loss but the strange tenderness that grew in its place. Without the weight of our shared history, the old arguments and disappointments simply fell away. He no longer remembered that I had dropped out of law school, or that we had not spoken for nearly two years after my mother's funeral. He only knew that someone kind was sitting with him, bringing him tea, and listening to his stories about a childhood farm in Ohio that I had never heard about before.

In forgetting me, he had somehow made room to know me again.

The rain kept falling, on and off, through most of that autumn. I would sit by the window in the evenings while he slept, listening to it drum softly on the roof, and I would try to memorize everything: the way he hummed when he buttered toast, the crooked part in his silver hair, the exact sound of his laugh.

Someone, I thought, has to remember. And now, it would have to be me.
