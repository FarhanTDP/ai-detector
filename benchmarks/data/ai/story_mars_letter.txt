Dear Mom,

It has been 214 days since we landed on Mars, and I still can't get used to the color of the sky. Everyone back home imagines it as red, but in the afternoon it is more of a pale butterscotch, soft and hazy, like an old photograph. At sunset, strangely, it turns blue. Dr. Okafor explained the science to me twice, something about dust particles scattering light differently than on Earth, but honestly I prefer to think of it as the planet's way of saying goodnight.

Life in the habitat has settled into a routine. We wake up at 0600, check the oxygen scrubbers, and review the day's tasks over reconstituted oatmeal that tastes faintly of cardboard. I spend most mornings in the greenhouse module. You would be proud of me. The girl who killed every houseplant you ever bought her is now responsible for feeding six people on another planet. The lettuce is thriving, the potatoes are stubborn, and the strawberries are, well, a work in progress.

The hardest part is not the isolation or the cold, although the cold is relentless. It is the silence. On Earth, there is always some kind of noise in the background: traffic, birds, the neighbor's dog barking at nothing. Here, when you step outside in your suit, the only sounds are your own breathing and the soft hum of the life support system. It makes you realize how much of the world you used to take for granted.

Last week, we had our first major dust storm. It lasted four days, and for most of that time we could barely see more than a few meters beyond the windows. The solar panels were covered, so we had to ration power. We played cards by the dim glow of the emergency lights and told stories about home. Commander Reyes told us about the summer she spent working on her uncle's fishing boat in Alaska. I told everyone about the time you drove three hours in a snowstorm just to bring me soup when I had the flu in college. They all agreed that you sound like a legend.

I think about that a lot out here. About the small, quiet ways people take care of each other. It turns out that surviving on Mars is not so different from surviving anywhere else. You need food, shelter, and air, of course. But mostly you need people who will show up for you when the storm rolls in.

The next supply ship arrives in three months. I have asked them to include a packet of tomato seeds and, if possible, a jar of your homemade strawberry jam. I know it is a long shot, but a girl can dream.

Please give Dad a hug for me, and tell Biscuit that I miss him, even if he still chews my shoes.

All my love from 140 million miles away,
Sarah
