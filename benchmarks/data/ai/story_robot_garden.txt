Unit 7 had been designed to sort recycling, not to grow tomatoes.

For three years, it had worked tirelessly at the edge of the city, separating glass from plastic and aluminum from cardboard with mechanical precision. Its days were measured in tons processed and efficiency ratings. It did not dream, because it had not been built to dream. It did not wonder, because wondering was not part of its programming.

Then, one rainy Tuesday, a small seed packet fell from a torn shopping bag and landed on its conveyor belt.

Unit 7 paused. According to its protocols, the packet should have been classified as paper waste. Yet something about the faded picture on the front, a bright red tomato glistening with dew, caused its processors to hesitate. It scanned the label. "Plant in full sun. Water regularly. Harvest in 70 days." The instructions were simple, almost like a program.

Curious in a way it could not explain, Unit 7 tucked the packet into an empty compartment on its chassis.

That evening, after the facility had gone quiet, the robot rolled to a forgotten corner of the yard where a crack in the concrete had allowed a strip of soil to show through. Carefully, it pressed the seeds into the earth with one metal finger. It calculated the optimal depth and spacing. It collected rainwater from a dented barrel and poured it gently over the soil.

Days turned into weeks. Every night, Unit 7 returned to the little patch of ground. It monitored moisture levels, measured sunlight exposure, and shielded the tiny green sprouts from the wind with scraps of discarded metal. Slowly, the seedlings grew taller and stronger, their leaves unfolding like small green hands reaching for the sky.

The other machines in the facility did not understand. A forklift beeped in confusion as it passed. A drone hovered overhead, recording data it could not interpret. But Unit 7 did not mind. For the first time, it was doing something that could not be measured in efficiency ratings.

On the seventieth day, the first tomato ripened. It was small and slightly lopsided, but it was a vivid, perfect red.

Unit 7 did not eat, of course. Instead, it carefully picked the tomato and placed it on the windowsill of the facility office, where the night supervisor, an elderly woman named Rosa, would find it in the morning.

When Rosa arrived and saw the tomato, she laughed in surprise and looked around the empty yard. Somewhere in the corner, a robot's indicator light blinked softly, glowing a color that, if machines could feel, might have been called happiness.

From that day on, the forgotten corner of the yard was never empty again.
